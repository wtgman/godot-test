# Finding the angles

How to get from a piece of content to a menu worth choosing from.

This is the hard part of the job. Turning a decided idea into an activity is
mechanical, and the toolkit does it. Deciding which activity, out of the ones
nobody has thought of yet, is the work.

---

## Why the first idea is usually the wrong one

The idea that comes first is almost always the one the prose already states.
The content lists five stages, so you draw five boxes. The content mentions a
price, so you write the price larger.

That adds nothing. The learner already read the sentence. It costs load time,
it costs a screen reader user a detour, and it teaches learners that the
graphics in this course can be skipped.

Worse, it is passive. A learner who looks at a diagram of five boxes has done
nothing with the idea. The activities worth building make the learner commit to
something first: a guess, an order, a category, a reply. The commitment is what
makes the explanation that follows land.

So the question is never "how do I illustrate this paragraph". It is **"what is
true about this content that a learner cannot see from reading it, and what
could they do that would make them see it?"**

---

## Start with the analysis

Before any idea, write down four things. The proposal requires them, and every
candidate should say which of them it works on.

**Key ideas.** One to six. The things a learner would be lost without, not a
summary of the content. If you have more than six, you are summarising.

**The threshold idea.** The one that, once understood, changes how the rest
looks, and is hard to un-see. For grief: the five are a vocabulary, not a map.
For the Instagram deal: platforms were bought for users and attention, not
profit. It is often not what the content spends longest on, and it is usually
the best thing to build a during-class activity around.

**Misconceptions.** What learners arrive believing that is wrong, written as the
belief and what is actually the case. The source never mentions them, because
the person who wrote it does not hold them. Ask the teacher if you are unsure:
they hear them every term.

**Know or do.** What learners need to know, and what they need to be able to do.
Content that is mostly knowing suits predict, step-through and cards. Content
that is mostly doing suits sort, order, scenario and explore. Most content is
both, which is why a proposal is an arc rather than one activity.

## Ten moves for finding an angle

Run these against any content. Most will produce nothing. Two or three will
produce something the teacher had not considered, which is the point of
proposing rather than building.

### 1. Compared to what

A number alone means nothing. Find what it should sit beside, and hide it.

> One billion dollars for Instagram. Compared to what? YouTube went for more six
> years earlier. WhatsApp went for nineteen times as much two years later.

→ `estimate`, with the comparison deals visible and the one that matters hidden.
This is the highest-yield move and the most neglected.

### 2. The rate hiding in the sequence

A timeline carries dates. The gaps between them carry a story nobody wrote
down.

> Launch to billion-dollar acquisition: eighteen months.

→ `predict` with a number response ("how long from launch to sale?"), then a
timeline in the reveal. Or a `stepthrough` of the timeline that pauses on the
gap.

### 3. The ratio nobody computed

Two figures in the source imply a third that is more striking than either.

> One billion dollars, thirteen staff. About seventy-seven million per person.

Only if the ratio means something. Dividing two unrelated numbers produces a
figure that looks like a fact and is not.

→ `explore` with a formula, so the learner computes it for other deals too. Or
a `predict` number response.

### 4. The misconception to pre-empt

What do learners arrive believing that is wrong? This is often the single most
valuable activity in a topic.

> Everyone arrives believing grief runs from denial to acceptance, in order.
> The content does not say that. Correcting it is the lesson.

→ `predict`. Let them commit to the wrong belief, then show them. Asking first
is what makes the correction stick: a misconception that is only contradicted
tends to survive, one that the learner has just used and seen fail does not.

### 5. The decision the learner will actually face

Content describes. Practice decides. Find the moment where the learner will
have to choose, and build that instead of the description.

> Not "the five responses are X". Instead: a family member says this in the
> corridor. What do you say back?

→ `scenario` when the decision unfolds in a conversation, `sort` when it is a
matter of recognising which case this is.

### 6. The invisible whole

Where does all of something go? Time, marks, budget, effort, a shift.

> A hundred marks in this unit. Where do they come from?

→ `estimate` ("how much of the mark is the exam?") with a waffle in the reveal,
or a waffle figure on its own.

### 7. What had to be true first

Dependencies that the prose lists in narrative order rather than causal order.

> The permit takes six weeks and nothing on site can start without it. Every
> other task is arranged around that fact.

→ `stepthrough` of a gantt, built task by task, pausing to ask what each one is
waiting for. Or `order`.

### 8. The counterfactual

What if the number were different? If the content contains a relationship rather
than a fact, the learner should be able to push on it.

> What happens to every orbit if the star is three times heavier?

→ `explore`, with challenges that make the learner hit a target rather than
just play.

### 9. What an expert notices that a novice does not

Expertise is largely perceptual. Make the noticing explicit, then practise it.

> A practitioner hears "if I get him walking every day, that will buy more time"
> and hears bargaining. A student hears optimism.

→ `sort` of real cases into categories, with the cue explained each time. A
`labelled` figure when the noticing is visual.

### 10. The thing that is easy to get wrong in the right order

If sequence or timing is a skill rather than a fact, make learners commit to it
and then show the model.

> When does each dish go on so everything arrives hot at once?

→ `order` with `"mode": "timing"`, which draws each step as a bar against the
serving time as the learner types.

## Questions worth asking

Ask at most six. More is an interview, and it will not get answered. Only ask
what would **change what you build**. If both answers lead to the same menu, do
not ask.

The ones that most often change it:

1. **Purpose.** Recognising, recalling, applying, deciding, or critiquing?
   Recognise and recall suit sort and cards. Apply and decide suit scenario,
   order and explore. Critique suits predict and step-through.
2. **The one thing.** If a learner remembers one thing in a year, what should it
   be? Often not what the content emphasises, and usually the threshold idea.
3. **The prior belief.** What do learners arrive believing that is wrong? Feeds
   move 4 directly.
4. **Data.** Do you have the figures, do they need sourcing, or do they not exist?
   Ask early, because it decides which candidates are possible.
5. **Assessment.** Is it assessed? A scored pattern delivered through SCORM can
   report to the gradebook, with a pass mark.
6. **Placement.** Before, during or after class? A before-class activity hooks
   and does not teach. A during-class one is built for the teacher to talk over.
   An after-class one has to stand on its own.

Give options where the answer is a choice between named alternatives. A
question with three options gets answered. An open question gets skipped.

## Data provenance

Every candidate declares where its content comes from before anyone commits to
building it. The validator enforces it.

| Status | Means | Blocks release |
|---|---|---|
| `none-needed` | Structural. Everything is in the source already | No |
| `in-source` | Every figure comes from the supplied content and can be checked against it | No |
| `needs-teacher` | The shape is right, the figures or cases must come from the unit guide, the cohort or the workplace | Yes |
| `needs-research` | The figures are public but must be found, cited and checked | Yes |
| `unavailable` | Proposed, then ruled out because the data does not exist or cannot be separated | Yes |

A blocking status must say exactly what is missing. "Needs figures" is not
something a teacher can act on. "User counts at the time of each deal, labelled
as registered or monthly active" is.

`unavailable` matters most, because it saves someone from publishing a
fabrication.

> "Show Instagram's profit over time" sounds reasonable. Meta has never reported
> it. It reports two segments, not individual apps, so every figure online is a
> third-party model. Charting it as profit presents a guess as a disclosure.

The right response is not to refuse the idea. It is to say what does not exist,
then offer the honest version: an estimate attributed to whoever made it and
labelled as an estimate. Reading the difference between a reported figure and a
modelled one is itself worth teaching.

**Never soften `needs-research` into `in-source` by filling the gap from
memory.** A figure recalled rather than sourced is a fabrication with a
confident tone.

## Sketches

Give every strong candidate a sketch: a real learning-object spec, built as a
draft, that runs in the gallery. Trying an activity as a learner for thirty
seconds tells a teacher more than a paragraph describing it.

- Mark it `"sketch": true` when any content is placeholder. The draft banner
  says so on the page.
- Use real content where you have it, with its sources marked `to-check`. It
  still builds as a draft.
- A candidate whose content does not exist yet has no sketch. The gallery says
  what it is waiting for.

The validator refuses a sketch that would build as finished, so a preview can
never be mistaken for the real thing.

## Recommending an arc

Recommend one to three candidates. Not none, which pushes the decision back onto
the teacher. Not most of the list, which is the same thing in disguise.

Where you can, recommend an arc across the week:

- **Before class**, something that makes learners want to know: a predict or an
  estimate. They arrive with a guess they want to check.
- **During class**, something the teacher explains with: a step-through, a
  scenario the room votes through, or an explore model on the projector.
- **After class**, something to practise with: sort, order or cards.

Pick on this order:

1. What corrects a misconception beats what adds information.
2. What a learner will do beats what a learner will look at.
3. What can be built honestly today beats what needs data nobody has.
4. What the page does not already say in prose beats a restatement.

Say why in one sentence. "This is the one they will remember" is a better
reason than "this is the most comprehensive".

## Recording what you ruled out

The `rejected` list is not padding. On well-known content it is often the most
useful part of the proposal, because it stops the obvious-but-wrong idea being
proposed again next term by someone else.

Give the reason in terms of what it would teach, not taste:

> **The five stages as a numbered process flow.** Five numbered boxes with
> arrows asserts a fixed order, which is exactly the misreading this topic has
> to correct. A diagram states it more persuasively than prose, so building it
> would teach the misconception.

Three things belong here almost every time:

- The visual the content most obviously suggests, if it is wrong.
- Anything whose data turned out not to exist.
- Anything that would need more than about eight items, because that is where a
  visual stops teaching and starts listing.
