The Instagram acquisition

The source is a short history of Instagram: launched October 2010, bought by Facebook in April 2012 for a headline one billion US dollars, with thirteen staff and no revenue. The figures below come from contemporary reporting and filings, listed in research.md, and every one is marked to check until someone has opened the original.

Who it is for. Social media and digital marketing learners meeting platform economics for the first time.

What matters here

  • Facebook paid a headline US$1 billion for a company with thirteen staff and no revenue.
  • A price only means something next to other prices. Against later deals it looks small, against its revenue it looks absurd.
  • Big technology companies buy rivals to grow, and also to stop a rival growing.
  • Headline prices are not what was paid. Deals paid in shares change value before they close.

The idea that unlocks the rest. Platforms were bought for users and attention, not for profit. Once learners see that, a billion dollars for a company with no revenue stops looking like a mistake and starts looking like a strategy.

What learners need to know. What Facebook paid, what Instagram had at the time, and how that compares to other acquisitions of the period.
What they need to be able to do. Put an acquisition price in context and explain the strategic reason a company might pay it.

What learners often arrive believing

The beliefWhat is actually the case
Facebook bought Instagram because it was making money.It had no revenue at all. Facebook was paying for its users and its growth.
The announced price is what was paid.The deal was mostly Facebook shares, which fell before it closed, so it was worth roughly US$715 million by then.
A bigger price means a better deal.Tumblr cost about the same as Instagram and was later sold for a tiny fraction of it.

Questions that would change what I build

1. Is the lesson about valuation, or about competitive strategy?

The same billion dollars supports two different lessons. Valuation asks how you price something with no revenue. Strategy asks why you pay anything at all to stop a rival. Options 1 to 3 suit either. Option 4 is strategy.

  • Valuation
  • Competitive strategy
  • Both, across the week

2. Do you want figures kept to 2012, or brought up to date?

A 2012-only lesson is fully sourceable from the announcements. Bringing it to today means third-party estimates, because Meta has never published Instagram's revenue or profit separately. That is a teaching point in itself, but it changes what can be cited.

  • 2012 only, everything citable
  • Up to date, with estimates clearly labelled

The options

1

Option 1. What was Instagram worth?

  • Recommended
  • Estimate, then compare
  • Before class
  • Needs sourcing and checking
  • low effort
The angle
YouTube, Tumblr and LinkedIn are shown. Instagram and WhatsApp are hidden. Learners drag each hidden bar to their guess, then see the real prices land against their guesses.
Afterwards
Place the Instagram price among the deals of its time, and say why the headline price is not the whole story.
Works on
The belief that the announced price is what was paid.
Why this format
Committing to a guess and then seeing the gap turns a figure into a felt sense of size, which reading the number never does.
What it needs
Eight prices from announcements, filings and reporting. Tumblr's 2019 sale price is reported, never disclosed.
Try the sketch as a learner

Option 2. How Instagram grew

  • Recommended
  • Step by step
  • During class
  • Needs sourcing and checking
  • low effort
The angle
The timeline built one turning point at a time at the front of the room, pausing on each to ask what it was responding to: a rival, or a change in how people used their phones.
Afterwards
Explain Instagram's growth as a series of responses to competitors and to phones.
Works on
The threshold idea: platforms compete for attention.
Why this format
Showing one part at a time, with a sentence about each, removes the work of finding where to start and what to read next.
What it needs
Launch dates for video, Stories and Reels, checked against primary sources.

Option 3. Why buy a rival?

  • Recommended
  • Sort it
  • After class
  • Needs sourcing and checking
  • medium effort
The angle
Short descriptions of real technology acquisitions, sorted by the main reason for the purchase: to grow, to stop a rival, or to acquire people and technology. The contested ones make the best discussion.
Afterwards
Name the strategic reason behind an acquisition and back it with evidence.
Works on
The skill of explaining why a company pays what it pays.
Why this format
Recognition is learned from contrasting examples, not from definitions, and the reason given for each item teaches the cue to look for.
What it needs
A reason for each deal, sourced to reporting or filings rather than assumed. Where the reason is contested, say so in the feedback.

Option 5. What was each user worth?

  • Explore the model
  • During class

A price divided by users at the time of sale, with sliders for both. Learners set Instagram and WhatsApp side by side and see that the price per user is closer than the headline prices suggest.

User counts at the time of each deal, from the announcements, each labelled as registered or monthly active users, since the two are not comparable.

Considered and ruled out

  • Instagram's profit over time. The data does not exist. Meta reports two segments, Family of Apps and Reality Labs, not individual apps, so there is no profit figure to chart. Anything found online is a third-party model. Charting it as profit would present a guess as a disclosure.
  • The acquisition as a process flow. The negotiation is not documented well enough to draw without inventing steps, and the interesting part is the judgement, not the sequence.
  • Growth of every social platform since 2004. Too many series and too many definitions of a user, and it answers no question this lesson is asking.
