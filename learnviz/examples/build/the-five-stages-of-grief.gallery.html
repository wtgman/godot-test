The five stages of grief

Well-known content with no supplied source material. Kübler-Ross named five responses in 1969 after interviewing people who were dying. Almost every learner arrives already holding a flattened version of it, so the teaching problem is not introducing the model. It is correcting what learners already believe, and turning it into something they can use in a conversation.

Who it is for. Community services and aged care learners who will support grieving residents and families on placement.

What matters here

  • Kübler-Ross described five responses to loss: denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance.
  • They are not a sequence. People move between them, skip some, and show several at once.
  • Each response sounds different in what a person says, and a worker can learn to hear it.
  • The worker's job is to keep the conversation open and stay within their role, not to move someone along.

The idea that unlocks the rest. The five are a vocabulary for what someone might be feeling, not a map of where they should be. Once learners see that, they stop trying to move people to the next stage and start listening to the one in front of them.

What learners need to know. The five responses, and what each can sound like in everyday speech.
What they need to be able to do. Respond to a grieving family member in a way that keeps the conversation open, without arguing, over-promising or stepping outside their role.

What learners often arrive believing

The beliefWhat is actually the case
Grief happens in five stages, in order.Kübler-Ross and later writers stressed that the responses do not come in a fixed order, and many people never show some of them.
Acceptance means feeling better.Acceptance is engaging with what is real. It can be very sad.
An angry family member is angry at me.Anger is often aimed at whoever is in the room. It is rarely about that person.
If someone is in denial, I should gently correct them.Denial often protects someone while the news sinks in. Arguing with it usually closes the conversation.

Questions that would change what I build

1. Is this for recognising grief on placement, or for critiquing the model itself?

Recognition needs practice hearing the responses and answering them. Critique needs the history of how the model was misread. Both are worth building, but the options below lean to recognition, because that is what placement students need first.

  • Recognition, for use on placement
  • Critique, as an example of a contested model
  • Both

2. Does your unit prescribe a specific grief model?

Many current units teach dual process or continuing bonds alongside or instead of stage theory. If yours does, option 6 becomes worth building and the others should mention it.

  • Kübler-Ross only
  • Kübler-Ross plus a named alternative
  • Not sure, I will check the unit guide

3. Can someone who works in aged care read the constructed examples before release?

1

Option 1. Put the five stages in order

  • Recommended
  • Predict, then check
  • Before class
  • Needs sourcing and checking
  • low effort
The angle
Before they read anything, learners put the five in the order they think people go through them. Almost everyone produces the textbook sequence, and the reveal shows why that sequence is the misconception.
Afterwards
Say why the five responses are not a sequence, in their own words.
Works on
The belief that grief happens in five stages, in order.
Why this format
Generating an answer first makes the real one stick, and surfaces the belief the learner walked in with.
What it needs
Check the reveal's wording against On Death and Dying (1969) and On Grief and Grieving (2005).

Option 2. The five responses, one at a time

  • Recommended
  • Step by step
  • During class
  • Needs content from you

The angle
Taken one response per step at the front of the room: what it can sound like, what the person may need, and the well-meant reply that shuts the conversation down.
Afterwards
Name each response from what someone says, and know one reply to avoid for each.
Works on
The threshold idea: the five are a vocabulary, not a map.
Why this format
Showing one part at a time, with a sentence about each, removes the work of finding where to start and what to read next.
What it needs

The angle
Nine things a family member might say, each sorted into the response it shows most clearly. A first miss gets a nudge, a second gets the answer and the cue to listen for.
Afterwards
Recognise each response from real speech rather than from a definition.
Works on
Hearing the responses in everyday speech, including the ones that sound hopeful.
Why this format
Recognition is learned from contrasting examples, not from definitions, and the reason given for each item teaches the cue to look for.

The angle
A resident's daughter stops the learner in the corridor and asks them to get the scan redone. Each choice leads somewhere different, with coaching on why.

The belief that denial should be corrected, and the skill of keeping a conversation open.
Why this format
Judgement is learned by practising it in context, and in a scenario the wrong call costs nothing and teaches the most.

Option 5. What to say instead

  • Recall practice
  • After class

The angle
Each card shows something kind people often say to someone who is grieving. The learner thinks of a better reply before turning it over.
Afterwards
Have a better phrase ready for the moments that usually go wrong.
Why this format
Recalling an answer strengthens memory far more than rereading it, and meeting the misses again a few cards later spaces the practice.
What it needs
Check the replacement phrases suit the tone and cultural context of your placements.

Considered and ruled out

  • The five stages as a numbered process flow. This is the obvious visual and it is the wrong one. Five numbered boxes with arrows asserts a fixed order, which is exactly the misreading this topic has to correct. A diagram states it more persuasively than prose, so building it would teach the misconception.
  • The five stages as a cycle. Better than a process, but still wrong. A ring with arrows asserts that the responses recur in a fixed order. They recur in no order at all.
  • Proportion of bereaved people showing each response. There is no agreed figure to put in it. Studies use different populations, definitions and time windows. A chart would manufacture a precision the evidence does not support.
