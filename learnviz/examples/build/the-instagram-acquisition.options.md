# Activity options: The Instagram acquisition

The source is a short history of Instagram: launched October 2010, bought by Facebook in April 2012 for a headline one billion US dollars, with thirteen staff and no revenue. The figures below come from contemporary reporting and filings, listed in research.md, and every one is marked to check until someone has opened the original.

**Who it is for.** Social media and digital marketing learners meeting platform economics for the first time.

## What matters here

- Facebook paid a headline US$1 billion for a company with thirteen staff and no revenue.
- A price only means something next to other prices. Against later deals it looks small, against its revenue it looks absurd.
- Big technology companies buy rivals to grow, and also to stop a rival growing.
- Headline prices are not what was paid. Deals paid in shares change value before they close.

**The idea that unlocks the rest.** Platforms were bought for users and attention, not for profit. Once learners see that, a billion dollars for a company with no revenue stops looking like a mistake and starts looking like a strategy.

**What learners often arrive believing**

- *Facebook bought Instagram because it was making money.* It had no revenue at all. Facebook was paying for its users and its growth.
- *The announced price is what was paid.* The deal was mostly Facebook shares, which fell before it closed, so it was worth roughly US$715 million by then.
- *A bigger price means a better deal.* Tumblr cost about the same as Instagram and was later sold for a tiny fraction of it.

## 2 questions before I build

**1. Is the lesson about valuation, or about competitive strategy?**

The same billion dollars supports two different lessons. Valuation asks how you price something with no revenue. Strategy asks why you pay anything at all to stop a rival. Options 1 to 3 suit either. Option 4 is strategy.

- Valuation
- Competitive strategy
- Both, across the week

**2. Do you want figures kept to 2012, or brought up to date?**

A 2012-only lesson is fully sourceable from the announcements. Bringing it to today means third-party estimates, because Meta has never published Instagram's revenue or profit separately. That is a teaching point in itself, but it changes what can be cited.

- 2012 only, everything citable
- Up to date, with estimates clearly labelled

## Recommended arc

- **Before class.** 1. What was Instagram worth?
- **During class.** 2. How Instagram grew
- **After class.** 3. Why buy a rival?

## The options

### 1. What was Instagram worth?

Estimate, then compare · Before class · Needs sourcing and checking · low effort · sketch to try

**The angle.** YouTube, Tumblr and LinkedIn are shown. Instagram and WhatsApp are hidden. Learners drag each hidden bar to their guess, then see the real prices land against their guesses.

**What a learner can do after it.** Place the Instagram price among the deals of its time, and say why the headline price is not the whole story.

**Works on.** The belief that the announced price is what was paid.

**What it needs.** Eight prices from announcements, filings and reporting. Tumblr's 2019 sale price is reported, never disclosed.

### 2. How Instagram grew

Step by step · During class · Needs sourcing and checking · low effort · sketch to try

**The angle.** The timeline built one turning point at a time at the front of the room, pausing on each to ask what it was responding to: a rival, or a change in how people used their phones.

**What a learner can do after it.** Explain Instagram's growth as a series of responses to competitors and to phones.

**Works on.** The threshold idea: platforms compete for attention.

**What it needs.** Launch dates for video, Stories and Reels, checked against primary sources.

### 3. Why buy a rival?

Sort it · After class · Needs sourcing and checking · medium effort

**The angle.** Short descriptions of real technology acquisitions, sorted by the main reason for the purchase: to grow, to stop a rival, or to acquire people and technology. The contested ones make the best discussion.

**What a learner can do after it.** Name the strategic reason behind an acquisition and back it with evidence.

**Works on.** The skill of explaining why a company pays what it pays.

**What it needs.** A reason for each deal, sourced to reporting or filings rather than assumed. Where the reason is contested, say so in the feedback.

### 4. What was Instagram earning?

Predict, then check · Before class · Needs sourcing and checking · low effort · sketch to try

**The angle.** Learners commit to a guess at Instagram's annual revenue when Facebook bought it. The answer, nothing at all, is the hook for the whole topic.

**What a learner can do after it.** Explain what Facebook was paying for, if not revenue.

**Works on.** The belief that Facebook bought Instagram because it was making money.

**What it needs.** The staff count, revenue and user figures at the time of sale, from contemporary reporting.

### 5. What was each user worth?

Explore the model · During class · Needs sourcing and checking · medium effort

**The angle.** A price divided by users at the time of sale, with sliders for both. Learners set Instagram and WhatsApp side by side and see that the price per user is closer than the headline prices suggest.

**What a learner can do after it.** Compare acquisitions of different sizes on a like-for-like measure.

**Works on.** The belief that a bigger price means a better deal.

**What it needs.** User counts at the time of each deal, from the announcements, each labelled as registered or monthly active users, since the two are not comparable.

## Considered and ruled out

- **Instagram's profit over time.** The data does not exist. Meta reports two segments, Family of Apps and Reality Labs, not individual apps, so there is no profit figure to chart. Anything found online is a third-party model. Charting it as profit would present a guess as a disclosure.
- **The acquisition as a process flow.** The negotiation is not documented well enough to draw without inventing steps, and the interesting part is the judgement, not the sequence.
- **Growth of every social platform since 2004.** Too many series and too many definitions of a user, and it answers no question this lesson is asking.
