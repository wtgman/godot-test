# Visual options: The five stages of grief

Well-known content with no supplied source material. Kübler-Ross named five responses in 1969 after interviewing people who were dying. Almost every learner arrives already holding a flattened version of it, so the teaching problem here is not introducing the model, it is correcting what they already believe.

**Who it is for.** Community services and aged care learners who will sit with grieving clients on placement.

## Recommended

### 1. Recognising the responses in what someone says

`comparison` · No data needed · low effort

**The angle.** Skip the definitions. Put each response beside the words a worker actually hears, what the person needs, and the well-meant reply that shuts the conversation down. The 'what not to say' column is the one that changes behaviour, because every phrase in it is one a kind person would offer.

**What a learner can do after it.** Learners can recognise a response from a client's words and choose a reply that keeps the conversation open.

### 2. How the five stages became a sequence they were never meant to be

`timeline` · Data is in the source · low effort

**The angle.** The history is the correction. 1969, about people facing their own death. Borrowed for bereavement in the 1970s and flattened into steps. Kübler-Ross saying plainly in 2005 that they are not a linear timeline. Learners stop treating the misreading as a detail and see it as the whole problem.

**What a learner can do after it.** Learners can explain what Kübler-Ross actually claimed and why the five-step version misleads families.

**What it needs.** Dates and titles are from the two books and are checkable. The line about research not finding a fixed sequence is stated generally and needs a citation from your reading list before publishing.

## Also possible

### 3. What a worker should do in the first ten minutes

`process` · Needs figures from you · low effort

**The angle.** Turn the model into an actual procedure. Not the stages, which are not a procedure, but the response: observe, name nothing, ask an open question, sit with the silence, escalate if risk appears. This is legitimately a process because it is what the worker does, in order.

**What a learner can do after it.** Learners can carry out a first response to a distressed client without needing to classify them first.

**What it needs.** Your service's actual escalation step. Who does a student on placement tell, and how fast. A generic 'escalate to a supervisor' is the gap that leaves a student stuck.

### 4. Kübler-Ross against a model your unit also teaches

`comparison` · Needs figures from you · medium effort

**The angle.** Set stage theory beside dual process or continuing bonds on the same criteria: what it says grief is, what it predicts, what it gets used for, where it fails. Learners stop seeing one canonical model and start seeing a field with an argument in it.

**What a learner can do after it.** Learners can say what each model is useful for and name a situation where stage theory would mislead them.

**What it needs.** Which alternative model your unit prescribes. I will not pick one for you, because the comparison is only useful if it matches what is assessed.

### 5. Reading a client's words, as a practice activity

`sequencer` · Needs figures from you · high effort

**The angle.** Recognition is a skill, so make them practise it rather than read a table. Give a series of things a client says across several weeks and ask which response each one shows, including one that repeats a response the client had already moved past. That repeat is the whole lesson.

**What a learner can do after it.** Learners can classify a real utterance and can say why an out-of-order response is normal rather than a relapse.

**What it needs.** Six to ten client utterances you are happy to use, ideally from your own practice or from the unit's case studies. Also note that the sequencer scores against a model answer, which suits timing better than it suits interpretation, so this may be better built as an H5P activity.

## Considered and ruled out

- **The five stages as a numbered process flow** (`process`). This is the obvious visual and it is the wrong one. Five numbered boxes with arrows asserts a fixed order, which is exactly the claim Kübler-Ross denied and the claim the literature spent decades correcting. A diagram states it more persuasively than prose, so building this would actively teach the misconception.
- **The five stages as a cycle** (`cycle`). Better than a process, but still wrong. A ring with directional arrows asserts that the responses recur in a fixed order. They recur in no order at all.
- **Proportion of bereaved people showing each response** (`waffle`). There is no agreed figure to put in it. Studies use different populations, different definitions and different time windows, and the honest summary is that most people do not pass through all five at all. A waffle chart here would manufacture a precision the evidence does not support.
