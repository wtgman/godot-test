Image Description

  • Main subject/content: How the five stages became a sequence they were never meant to be. A vertical timeline. Events run from earliest at the top to latest at the bottom, joined by a single vertical line.

    • 1969: On Death and Dying is published. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross describes five responses she observed while interviewing people who were dying. The subject is a person facing their own death, not a person grieving someone else's.
    • 1970s and 1980s: The model is borrowed for bereavement. It spreads into bereavement care, training and popular culture, applied to grieving a death rather than facing one. It is also flattened into five numbered steps along the way.
    • 2005: Kübler-Ross says plainly that they are not a sequence. In On Grief and Grieving, written with David Kessler, she states the responses are not stops on a linear timeline, that not everyone experiences all of them, and that they can recur.
    • 2000s onward: The evidence does not find a fixed order. Studies of bereaved people repeatedly fail to find everyone moving through a set sequence. The most common pattern reported is resilience rather than progression through stages.
    • 2019: A sixth response is proposed. David Kessler adds finding meaning, arguing the original five stop at acceptance and leave out what people do afterwards.
    • Now: The flattened version is still the one people arrive with. Most learners, and most families, meet grief as five stages in order. Expect to have to correct it, and expect a family to worry that someone is grieving wrongly because they are out of order.

  • Important visual details: Each event is a dot on the line. Turning points are drawn as a larger dot with a ring around it.

  • Relevant structure such as columns, layers, or groupings: 6 events, in chronological order. Each has a date, a heading, and a short explanation to the right of the line.
